\begin{abstract}
This work is relevant to researchers in continual learning, memory-augmented transformers, and biologically-inspired machine learning. We implement and empirically evaluate a biologically-faithful memory consolidation architecture for Large Language Models, drawing on the synaptic tagging-and-capture hypothesis, the Complementary Learning Systems framework, and hippocampal--neocortical replay during sleep. Our system, SLEEP, augments pretrained transformers with five interacting components: prediction-error tagging, direct-write key-value (KV) memory, protein-resource-allocation prioritization, generative replay, and LoRA-based consolidation under explicit safety constraints. Across 200 single-exposure synthetic facts, evaluated over five random seeds on each of four models spanning three families (Qwen2.5-7B and 1.5B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B), we systematically isolate where the architecture succeeds and fails. Direct-write KV memory injection produces a clear \emph{recognition} signal, but the same memories do not surface during free-form autoregressive \emph{recall}---a transformer analogue of the recognition--recall dissociation in cognitive psychology, and the recall floor reproduces at the same magnitude on every model we test. We then attempt to close the gap with a retrieval-aware warm-up phase that teaches the frozen model to use its memory during generation; across sixteen runs on the four models the warm-up learns its objective and improves recognition, but recall never moves, so we report it as a replicated negative result. Two further findings sharpen the picture. First, internal surprise-based validation confirms consolidations at a $\sim$93--100\% false-confirmation rate against a direct recall test, on every model. Second, running continual learning to ten cycles over nine seeded run-pairs shows that the safety machinery's apparent advantage is unstable: its damage-capping clip produces a preservation plateau whose existence is universal but whose level varies by three orders of magnitude across seeds, so fixed-horizon, single-run comparisons of continual-learning architectures are unreliable. We map five formalization amendments required for the architecture to function at scale and identify the consolidation step's stability--plasticity tradeoff as the central open problem.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models exhibit a form of architectural anterograde amnesia: pretrained weights are fixed at deployment, and any new information acquired during inference exists only within the context window, vanishing once the conversation ends. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) treats this as plumbing---append a database lookup to every query---rather than as a representational gap to close \citep{lewis2020retrieval}.

Biological memory faces an analogous problem and solves it through a structured pipeline: surprising experiences are flagged at the synapse by prediction-error-driven tags, those tags compete for a limited pool of plasticity-related proteins (PRPs), and the resulting episodic traces are gradually transferred from hippocampus to neocortex through replay during sleep. We review this literature in Section~\ref{sec:related} and Section~\ref{sec:background}; here we note only that the biological result is continuous, selective, single-exposure learning that does not catastrophically overwrite existing knowledge.

We propose SLEEP, a system that implements all five of these mechanisms---tagging, key-value (KV) memory, protein-resource allocation, sleep replay, and dual-weight consolidation---on Qwen2.5-7B, and that evaluates each component in isolation. Following a pre-registered formalization in which 36 design questions were resolved before any code was written, we instrument every stage of the pipeline and identify exactly where information is preserved, transformed, or lost. Rather than reporting a single end-to-end metric, this anatomy-of-failure framing localizes the boundary between the mechanisms that transfer to transformers and those that do not. Every headline result is reported across multiple random seeds.

Our results are mixed and specific. The encoding components work: direct-write KV memory injection produces a clear recognition signal on multiple-choice (MC) evaluation. The consolidation components do not: no configuration we tested achieves a Delayed Recall Accuracy (DRA) above $0.05$ while holding Base Capability Preservation (BCP) below $1.05$, and the recall floor sits at $\mathrm{DRA} \approx 0.006$ on every one of four models across three families, five seeds each. Because a persistent negative result invites the question ``can it be fixed?'', we implement and test the fix that the adjacent literature suggests---a retrieval-aware warm-up phase that teaches the model to use stored memory during generation. In sixteen runs across the four models it never moves recall. Finally, running the continual-learning comparison to ten cycles over nine seeded run-pairs shows that the safety machinery's apparent advantage is an artefact of horizon and seed: the constrained arm's damage plateau is universal in shape but lottery-like in level, and the cycle-10 ordering against naive fine-tuning flips seed to seed.

These findings yield the following contributions.

\paragraph{Contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(C\arabic*),itemsep=0.15em,topsep=0.2em,leftmargin=*]
    \item LoRA fast weights cannot encode at single exposure: $\alpha_{\mathrm{fast}} = 10^{-4}$ falls below the bfloat16 precision floor, and float32 testing shows the default magnitude is separately under-scaled at 7B.
    \item Direct-write KV memory injection produces recognition but not generative recall---a transformer analogue of the cognitive recognition--recall dissociation. The recall floor replicates at $\mathrm{DRA} \approx 0.006$--$0.007$ on four models across three families, five seeds each, while the same models follow in-context facts at up to $0.99$.
    \item Tags are pointers: storing only tagged tokens captures entity mentions without facts; episode-level storage is required.
    \item Internal validation passes while external recall fails: across all four models, $93$--$100\%$ of candidates that pass surprise-reduction fail a direct recall gate---a self-grading discrepancy not unique to SLEEP.
    \item No single-cycle Pareto operating point achieves $\mathrm{DRA} > 0.05$ with $\mathrm{BCP} < 1.05$ across our four-setting sweep.
    \item Fixed-horizon, single-run comparisons of continual-learning architectures are unreliable: over nine seeded ten-cycle run-pairs, SLEEP's clip produces a universal step-then-plateau preservation trajectory whose level varies by three orders of magnitude across seeds, flipping the cycle-10 ordering against naive LoRA seed-to-seed---while naive's recall is higher in 8 of 9 pairs.
    \item A retrieval-aware warm-up phase---the treatment the recognition--recall diagnosis implies---learns its training objective and improves recognition, but does not move recall in any of sixteen runs across the four models ($\leq$300 steps, general corpus).
\end{enumerate}

We do not claim SLEEP works as a deployable system. We claim something more specific: we show which biological mechanisms transfer to transformers (tagging, attention-based recognition, resource allocation), which do not in the form proposed (generative replay, LoRA consolidation under tight safety), which resist a principled repair attempt, and how strongly the answers depend on evaluation design. We release the implementation, formalization, experimental logs, and the five required amendments.
